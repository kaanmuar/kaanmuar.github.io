A guided tour

Your page is already talking. Here is how we listen.

Every public page says a lot before anyone writes a test: what it is built with, what search engines and AI assistants can read, what it tracks, how it protects visitors, who it leaves out, and how fast it answers. QA-lab URL Review writes that down for you, then proposes the test cases your team would want on paper before shipping.

This tour takes about five minutes. No sign-up is needed to read it.

The five-minute tour

From the first checkbox to the last export

  1. Read what the review will and will not do

    Every control on the review page stays off until you accept the disclaimers. They take a minute, and they explain the rules for Colombia, where the service is operated, and for the country of the site you submit. Why we ask: a review analyzes someone's public pages. You should know exactly what happens before a single address is opened.

  2. Say who is asking

    Sign in with email or an account you already have (Google, Microsoft, GitHub, Yahoo, or X); your email comes from that sign-in. Then give your name and the reason for the review. If the reason is your own site, sign in with an email at its domain. If you review a client's site, add the client's name, email, and website. Why we ask: there is a record of who asked, a site owner can ask us to stop and delete the report, and you can ask for a copy or deletion at any time.

  3. Paste one public address and pick your frameworks

    One https address, plus the frameworks your team uses: Playwright, Cypress, Robot, Selenium, WebdriverIO, CodeceptJS, Selenide, TestNG, Watir, Cucumber, Behave, Reqnroll, Jest, Vitest, Mocha, Appium, Maestro, JMeter, Gatling, or k6. The address box lends a hand while you type. It suggests well-known sites, asks “Did you mean…?” when a host does not exist, and after a slash it offers real pages from the site's own sitemap. Then, under Pages to review, choose how many pages, how many links deep, and which language to ask the site for. Tip: only care about the help center? Type example.com/help and leave Stay in this section on, and later pages stay under /help. Choose only the frameworks you would really run, too. Fewer make the export easier to read.

  4. Watch the analysis happen

    The review analyzes up to twenty pages, two links deep. It opens up to three of them in a headless browser from an EU address in Belgium, runs axe-core, checks phone and tablet widths, keeps three screenshots, and asks Google PageSpeed Insights to score the start page. A meter walks you through each phase, from connecting and the pages to the browser pass, the cases, the speed checks, and the score, and every log line appears the moment it happens. If a site is slow, the meter says so instead of going quiet. Good to know: in that browser it presses a cookie banner's reject button once and nothing else. Google receives the start address for the PageSpeed score and the Safe Browsing check, and the domain's registry is asked when it expires. A site whose bot protection refuses the plain request is analyzed in headless Chrome. Stop keeps what was already analyzed, and only your sign-in, on the device that started it, can press it.

  5. Read your report

    Six scores from 0 to 100: SEO, GEO, analytics, security, accessibility, and performance. Every check is marked fails now, at risk, passes, or needs a person, with the evidence, a fix, and an effort estimate. You also see your Google result, your share card, a map of the pages analyzed, and what to fix first. It also checks chat widgets and AI chatbots, the accessibility statement, imprint, and privacy pages, outdated front-end libraries, Google Safe Browsing, domain expiry and transfer lock, and MTA-STS, TLS-RPT, and BIMI for mail, and estimates the carbon of one visit. The proposed cases are built from what the review saw on your pages. The riskiest come first as P1 and P2, and each one names the real address it is about. Remember: signed-in runs are listed on your account page, and opening a report again asks for payment unless your account has an allowance. A shared link shows a blurred preview, and the full report opens only for the account that ran it.

  6. Take the cases home

    Export the proposed cases to a ZIP of automation scripts, Excel, TestRail, Xray, Zephyr Scale, Jira, Azure Test Plans, MantisBT, JSON, plain text, or PDF. The export also brings a GitHub Actions workflow and a fix kit with header files, a Report-Only content security policy, and drafts for structured data, llms.txt, security.txt, and DNS records. Need a single ticket instead of the whole list? Ask the report turns one finding into a GitHub issue or a Jira draft. The scripts are yours to run. The review never runs them against your site.

How to read the report

We reviewed ourselves first

These are the real scores from this site's own review on October 19, 2026: ten pages analyzed, 72 cases proposed, overall 86 out of 100. It is the same report you can open as the sample report. To compare, the live reports show the same review on more than two hundred public sites.

    What the four marks mean

    Fails now is a problem a visitor or a crawler can hit today. At risk works, but is fragile or incomplete. Passes is done well, and worth protecting with a test. Needs a person is a judgment a machine should not make for you, such as whether a third-party script is worth its weight.

    Where to start

    Read the fix-first list from the top. It ranks findings by how much they hurt and how little they cost to fix. Our own weakest area was security, at 54. The six fixes at the top were all response headers, a few lines of configuration each.

      Tips from real reviews

      Small things that keep showing up

      Security headers are cheap

      HSTS, nosniff, Referrer-Policy, and frame protection are configuration, not code. Many hosts let you add them in an afternoon.

      AI search reads without JavaScript

      If your key facts only appear after scripts run, many answer engines never see them. Put the facts in the HTML.

      Consent means before, too

      Check what loads before the visitor chooses. A tag that fires before Reject is the most common analytics finding.

      Your share card is a first impression

      A missing og:image turns every shared link into a grey box. The report shows the card people actually see.

      Accessibility is a test, not a feeling

      axe-core findings come with the element and the rule. Turn the passing checks into tests so they stay passing.

      Speed is per page

      The report shows milliseconds for each page in the order it analyzed them. The slowest page is rarely the home page.

      Questions people ask

      Before you start

      How much of a site does the first review analyze?

      Your first review is included: up to twenty pages, two links deep. A wider or deeper review asks for payment before it starts, and you see the amount first.

      Can I review a site I do not own?

      You can review any public https address with a real reason, and your sign-in records who asked. Scores and findings for public pages may be published as a Live Report, on GitHub, and on social media, unless you tick “Don't publish this review” on the request, never with the name, email, or reason of who asked. A site owner can ask this service to stop, to delete the report, and to take down its Live Report. The site's owner may get one email, at an address the site or its public records publish, saying a Live Report exists; it never says who asked. Please do not republish their content from a shared report.

      Is the score a certification?

      No. The report is a snapshot and a proposal from one public analysis. It is not proof that a site is safe, fast, or compliant.

      What about signed-in pages?

      The public review never signs in to your site. A verification that runs cases behind a login is a separate request to qa@carlosandmunoz.com, with a login you are allowed to use.

      Can my colleague open the link I shared?

      They see the blurred preview. A report opens in full only for the account that ran it, once that account signs in, and a review paid with a credit opens that report and no other. To pass the findings on, export the cases or print the report to PDF.

      How do I show a site is mine?

      The quick way: choose your own site as the reason and sign in with an email at that domain. The other way: on your account page, enter the host and press Get my token. Then add a DNS TXT record qa-lab-verify=<token>, or put <meta name="qa-lab-verify" content="<token>"> in the homepage head, and press Check now. A verified site gets the chat conversation in its reviews.

      My review failed. Now what?

      It happens. A site may be down, too slow, or behind a wall that day. We keep the log and look at every failure the next morning. When the cause clears, or we ship a fix for it, your review runs again. Once a person has checked the result, you get an email saying “Your review ran again and is in”, and that report is free.

      What does the site owner hear?

      At most one email per domain every six months, sent to an address the site or its public records already publish. It shows the health score, with buttons to claim the report, remove the site from Live Reports, or stop further notices. It never says who asked.

      Which languages can I read it in?

      The review page is written in English and can be read in Spanish, Portuguese, German, French, and Italian.
